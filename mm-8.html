Practical 1: Properties of alloys vs native metals

Aim

To investigate the properties of different alloys and native metals, and how composition affects their physical characteristics.

An alloy is a mixture of two or more elements, at least one a metal; its properties can be very different from pure metals. Native metals occur naturally in pure form (gold, copper, silver); they are useful but less versatile than alloys engineered for specific properties. You will analyse the density and hardness of three alloys and three native metals.

PracticePart 1, Q2–5
  1. Compare the densities of the alloys and native metals. Which are highest and lowest? What can you infer about their compositions?
  2. How does the hardness of the alloys compare with the native metals? Which would resist wear best?
  3. What can you infer about gold from its unusually high density?
  4. Which material would suit lightweight, durable engineering components, and why?
Model answer
  1. Gold is the densest (20 g/cm³), then silver (11.5) and copper (8.5); the alloys are much less dense (2.4–2.5 g/cm³), with Alloy C the lowest. The alloys must be made mostly of light metals (e.g. aluminium or magnesium alloys).
  2. All three alloys are harder (70–90) than the native metals (50–60). Alloy C (90) would be most resistant to wear and tear.
  3. Gold atoms are very heavy (high atomic mass) and closely packed, so gold is a pure, very dense element.
  4. Alloy C (or B): low density means lightweight and high hardness means durable, e.g. for aircraft or vehicle parts.
How did you go?
PracticePart 2: Research and applications
  1. Research typical uses of pure gold, copper and silver, and why they are valuable.
  2. Which alloy is most likely to substitute for copper in electrical applications, and why?
  3. Why are bronze and brass used more than pure copper or zinc in some applications?
  4. What are the advantages of silver for jewellery despite its lower hardness?
Model answer
  1. Gold: jewellery, electronics connectors (doesn't corrode, conducts well). Copper: electrical wiring, plumbing (excellent conductor, malleable). Silver: jewellery, electronics, mirrors (best conductor, very reflective). They are valuable because they are unreactive (don't corrode), attractive and relatively rare.
  2. The data gives no conductivity values, so this can't be decided for certain; alloys generally conduct less well than pure metals. A low-density alloy (e.g. an aluminium alloy like A) could be a lightweight substitute, as aluminium is used in overhead power lines.
  3. Bronze and brass are harder, stronger and more corrosion-resistant than the pure metals, and still easy to work (brass for locks, fittings and instruments; bronze for statues, bearings and marine parts).
  4. Silver is lustrous, easy to shape (malleable), unreactive (doesn't corrode, only tarnishes slowly) and cheaper than gold; it is often alloyed (sterling silver) to make it harder.

Practical 2: Reaction of metals with water

Safety

Wear goggles and aprons. Do not taste any chemicals. Reactive metals are corrosive and must not touch your skin. Test only one metal at a time.

Aim

To investigate the reactions of a variety of metals with water.

Method (students): clean a piece of zinc with sandpaper; fill a test tube with about 2 cm of water and add a few drops of phenolphthalein (turns pink if hydroxide ions are present); add the zinc with forceps and watch for gas bubbles and a pink colour. Repeat with magnesium, iron and copper in clean test tubes.

Teacher demonstration: in a half-filled water trough with phenolphthalein, away from students, small dried pieces of Li, Na, K and Ca are placed on the water one at a time.

The pink phenolphthalein colour shows a metal hydroxide (alkali) formed. Magnesium reacts very slowly with cold water but quickly with steam.

PracticeQuestions 1–4
  1. Why is it necessary to keep sodium and calcium under paraffin oil?
  2. Why were the metal surfaces cleaned with sandpaper?
  3. What is the function of the water trough (bath)?
  4. Using the periodic table, predict the reactivity of Cs, Rb, Mg and Ag with water.
Model answer
  1. To stop them reacting with oxygen and water vapour in the air.
  2. To remove the oxide layer on the surface, so the fresh metal can react with the water.
  3. A large volume of water lets the very reactive metals react safely (spreading the heat and keeping the reaction away from people); warm water can also be used to speed up slow reactions.
  4. Cs and Rb (Group 1, below K): even more violent, explosive. Mg: very slow reaction with cold water. Ag: no reaction.

Practical 3: Reaction of metals with acids

Safety

Wear goggles and aprons. All acids are corrosive. Do not taste any chemicals, and clean up spills with paper towel.

Aims

To investigate the reactions of metals with acids, and to develop a series of metals in order of reactivity.

Method: label four test tubes; clean each metal (zinc, magnesium, iron, copper) with sandpaper; add about 2 cm of 1 M acid (nitric, hydrochloric or sulfuric). Watch for bubbling or temperature change. If gas forms, lightly stopper the tube for a short time (not tightly), then hold a lit match to the mouth: a "pop" shows hydrogen. Your teacher then demonstrates the metals that showed no reaction in 10 M hydrochloric acid.

PracticeQuestions
  1. Why are some reactions heated in a water bath?
  2. Why is concentrated hydrochloric acid used for some metals?
  3. Combining Practicals 2 and 3, list the metals tested from most to least reactive.
Model answer
  1. Heating speeds up slow reactions so they can be observed in the lesson.
  2. Some metals (e.g. iron, lead) react too slowly with dilute acid to see; a higher concentration speeds up the reaction. (Copper still doesn't react with HCl, because it is below hydrogen.)
  3. Potassium > sodium > lithium > calcium > magnesium > zinc > iron > copper

Practical 4: Displacement of metals

Safety

Wear goggles and aprons. Use bench protectors.

Aim

To investigate and predict the reaction of a metal placed in a solution of a metal salt.

Of these two equations, only one happens on its own. Which?

  • Cu(s) + Zn(NO3)2(aq) → Zn(s) + Cu(NO3)2(aq)
  • Zn(s) + Cu(NO3)2(aq) → Cu(s) + Zn(NO3)2(aq)

Method: place each metal (iron, magnesium, zinc, copper) in separate test tubes, add 2 cm of each sulfate solution, label, leave until the end of the lesson, then record any reaction (✗ for none) and collect class results.

PracticeWhich reaction happens?
Explanation

Zinc is more reactive than copper, so zinc displaces copper. The reverse doesn't happen.

PracticeThe Law of Displacement

Write a conclusion and develop a rule to predict whether a displacement reaction will occur.

Model answer

A metal will displace any metal below it in the reactivity series from a solution of that metal's salt. A metal cannot displace a metal above it. E.g. magnesium displaced zinc, iron and copper; copper displaced none of the others.

Practical 5: Mighty Thermite (demonstration)

Thermite is a displacement reaction between a reactive metal powder (aluminium) and a metal oxide (usually red iron(III) oxide). Aluminium is more reactive, so it displaces the iron, producing molten iron. The reaction is extremely exothermic, reaching up to about 2500 °C. Thermite contains its own oxygen, so it can't be smothered and keeps burning even when wet.

Safety

This produces intense heat and light. It is done in a fume cupboard on heat-proof mats. Do not stare at the light.

A strip of magnesium is used as a fuse to provide the heat to start the reaction. Thermite is not explosive, but it heats a small area intensely, so it is used to weld metal, e.g. repairing broken railway tracks on site.

PracticeThermite equations and questions
  1. Write the overall equation, and the oxidation and reduction ionic equations.
  2. What is the function of the aluminium?
  3. Could copper be used instead of aluminium? Explain.
  4. Could copper oxide be used instead of iron oxide? Explain.
Model answer
  1. Overall: 2Al + Fe₂O₃ → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe. Oxidation: Al → Al³⁺ + 3e⁻. Reduction: Fe³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Fe.
  2. Aluminium is the reducing agent: it removes oxygen from iron oxide (it is oxidised) and releases a lot of heat.
  3. No: copper is less reactive than iron, so it can't displace iron from its oxide.
  4. Yes: aluminium is more reactive than copper, so it would displace copper from copper oxide (this reaction is also very exothermic).
How did you go?

Explanation

Magnesium → MgO + Cu: Mg + CuO → MgO + Cu. Calcium: Ca + MgO → CaO + Mg. Iron is less reactive than aluminium, so no reaction.

Practical 6: Extraction of copper from its ore

Safety

Wear goggles and aprons. Sulfuric acid is corrosive. Wash your hands at the end.

Aim

To extract copper and copper sulfate from a copper ore (malachite, CuCO3·Cu(OH)2), following the stages used in industry.

Method:

  1. Crush: grind the ore to a fine powder with a mortar and pestle.
  2. Dissolve: add 4 mL of sulfuric acid to the crushed ore, wait for frothing to stop, then keep adding small amounts until the frothing stops and the mineral has fully reacted.
  3. Filter: let it cool and settle, then filter, collecting the filtrate; examine the residue.
  4. Displacement: dilute the filtrate to 40 mL; place a clean iron nail and a piece of nickel in it for 5 minutes, then observe.
  5. Electrolysis: connect nickel electrodes to a 6 V DC supply in the solution for 5 minutes; your teacher tests the metallic electrode with nitric acid on a white tile.
PracticePractical 6 questions
  1. Describe the reaction of malachite with sulfuric acid.
  2. What colour is the filtrate? What is left in the filter paper?
  3. What is deposited on the nail? Explain any difference between iron and nickel.
  4. Why must electrolysis use DC? At which electrode is copper deposited, and what happens at the other?
  5. What happens when nitric acid is added to the electrode?
Model answer
  1. The green malachite fizzes (carbon dioxide is released from the carbonate) and dissolves, forming a blue solution of copper(II) sulfate.
  2. The filtrate is blue (copper sulfate solution); the residue is insoluble rock/sand impurities.
  3. Copper (a brown/pink coating) is deposited on the iron nail, because iron is more reactive than copper and displaces it. Nickel is only a little more reactive than copper, so it shows much less (or slower) deposition.
  4. DC keeps each electrode's charge constant, so copper always deposits on the same electrode. Copper is deposited at the cathode (−): Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. At the anode (+), bubbles of oxygen form (from water, as sulfate is not oxidised).
  5. Nitric acid reacts with copper, producing a blue-green solution (and brown gas), confirming the deposit is copper.
How did you go?
PracticeConclusion

Summarise the changes to the copper during each step of the extraction.

Model answer

The copper starts as copper carbonate in malachite ore; crushing increases the surface area. Sulfuric acid converts it to soluble copper(II) sulfate, separated from the impurities by filtering. Displacement by iron, or electrolysis (reduction at the cathode, Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu), turns the copper ions back into copper metal.
