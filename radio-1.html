Radioactivity
Chapter 1

Radioactivity and Isotopes

In this chapter (2 sections)
  1. The discovery of radioactivity
  2. Isotopes are the basis of radioactive behaviour

The discovery of radioactivity

The story begins in 1896, when Henri Becquerel discovered radioactivity by accident. He placed uranium compounds near some photographic plates (film), and the plates turned foggy. The compounds had other strange properties too: they glowed in the dark.

Because of Becquerel's work, we now know that radiation comes from radioactive atoms.

Radioactivity

Radioactivity, or radioactive decay, is the disintegration of unstable atomic nuclei. When an unstable nucleus disintegrates, it emits smaller particles, and sometimes electromagnetic radiation, until it forms a new, more stable nucleus.

The new, more stable nucleus has a different number of protons from the original, so it is actually a different element. Turning one element into another was once thought to be impossible, but it does happen. It is called transmutation.

Isotopes are the basis of radioactive behaviour

Many isotopes are stable, e.g. carbon-12, carbon-13, chlorine-35 and chlorine-37. Some, however, are unstable. Unstable isotopes are radioactive and are called radioisotopes.

  • Most elements on Earth have naturally occurring radioisotopes, e.g. carbon-14.
  • For some elements, every isotope is radioactive, e.g. uranium (U).
  • The elements from 93 to 118 are all man-made, and all of them are radioactive.
  • Radioisotopes can also be manufactured. For example, cobalt-60 is made artificially for use in cancer treatment.
